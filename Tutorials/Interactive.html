Stage 1: Creating Number Patterns (Modular Sequences)

🔢 What Are Modular Sequences?
A modular sequence is created by repeatedly multiplying and "wrapping around" at N—like a clock that goes from 12 back to 1.

We compute: x[k] = a^k mod N

The multiplicative order r of base a is how many steps until we return to 1.
x[k] = a^k mod N
Read aloud: "x at k equals a to the k, modulo N"
Concrete Example:
N = 997 (our "clock" wraps at 997)
r = 166 (pattern repeats every 166 steps)
bases = [7, 42, 103, 215] (four different starting numbers)

All four bases have the SAME order r = 166, meaning they all "play in the same key."
Each colored line shows a different base generating a^k mod 997. They look random but secretly share the same 166-step rhythm!

Stage 2: Phase Embedding on the Unit Circle

🎡 Converting Numbers to Circle Positions
We take each number from our sequence and map it to a point on a circle (the unit circle).

Small numbers → near 12 o'clock
Larger numbers → rotate clockwise around the circle
u[k] = exp(2πi · x[k]/N)
Read aloud: "u at k equals e to the two-pi-i times x of k over N"

Breaking it down:
• e = Euler's number (≈2.718)
• 2π = one complete circle
• i = imaginary unit (enables circular motion)
• x[k]/N = what fraction around the circle?
🌀 Why This Transformation Helps

• After: Smooth positions on a circle

This lets us use wave analysis tools (like FFT) that work better with smooth circular motion than discrete number jumps.

Stage 3: Phase-Coherent Averaging (The VRA Innovation)

🌊 What Is FFT?
FFT = "Fast Fourier Transform" (say: "eff-eff-tee")

Think of it as a machine that listens to a complex sound and tells you all the individual musical notes (frequencies) it contains. It converts time patterns into frequency patterns.
🎼 The Two Ways to Average
Method 1 - Incoherent (Traditional):
1. Measure volume of each singer separately
2. Average those volumes
Result: No louder than one singer ❌

Method 2 - Coherent (VRA's Secret Sauce):
1. Have singers harmonize together FIRST
2. THEN measure the combined volume
Result: Much louder when synchronized! ✅
S[f] = (1/M) × Σ U_i[f]
Power[f] = |S[f]|²
Read aloud: "S of f equals one over M times the sum of U sub i of f; Power of f equals absolute value of S of f, squared"

In plain English:
• M = number of bases (4 in our example)
• U_i[f] = FFT amplitude for base i at frequency f
• Σ = "sigma" means add them all up
• We average the complex waves FIRST, then square for power

The key difference: Traditional methods square each wave first, then average—losing the coherence boost!
📈 √M SNR Scaling

√M = "square root of M" (say: "square root of em")

When you use M synchronized sequences, the signal gets √M times stronger!

Examples:

Stage 4: Harmonic Detection with Validation

🎹 What Are Harmonic Bins?
When analyzing frequencies with FFT, we divide the spectrum into "bins"—like piano keys.

Harmonics are multiples of a fundamental frequency (like 100 Hz, 200 Hz, 300 Hz...).

Harmonic bins are the specific frequency slots where we EXPECT peaks based on order r.
Expected harmonic bins: B_k = ⌊k · N_zp / r⌋, k = 1,2,...,r-1
Validation radius: R = ⌊0.5 · log₂(N_zp)⌋
Read aloud: "B sub k equals floor of k times N-z-p over r; R equals floor of one-half times log base two of N-z-p"

Example calculation:
If N_zp = 2000 and r = 166:
• B₁ = ⌊1×2000/166⌋ = ⌊12.05⌋ = 12
• B₂ = ⌊2×2000/166⌋ = ⌊24.10⌋ = 24
• R = ⌊0.5×log₂(2000)⌋ = ⌊0.5×10.97⌋ = 5

So we expect a peak near bin 12 (±5 bins tolerance), another near bin 24 (±5 bins), etc.

Given data:
φ = {0.1, 0.2, 0.0, -0.1, 0.3}, M = 5
Step 1: Convert each phase to a vector (cos, sin)
e^(i·0.1) ≈ (0.9950, 0.0998)
e^(i·0.2) ≈ (0.9801, 0.1987)
e^(i·0.0) = (1.0000, 0.0000)
e^(i·-0.1) ≈ (0.9950, -0.0998)
e^(i·0.3) ≈ (0.9553, 0.2955)
Step 2: Sum all vectors componentwise
Real parts: 0.9950 + 0.9801 + 1.0000 + 0.9950 + 0.9553 = 4.9254
Imag parts: 0.0998 + 0.1987 + 0.0000 - 0.0998 + 0.2955 = 0.4942

Problem 5: Resolution Scaling Law
Read aloud: "Assume delta-omega-star of L equals k times L to the minus alpha. Given measured threshold at L equals 4096 where delta-omega-star equals 5 times 10 to the minus 3, and alpha equals 0.5, solve for k and predict at L equals 16384."
Δω*(L) = k · L^(-α)
Step 1: Solve for k using L = 4096
4096^(-0.5) = 1/√4096 = 1/64
5×10⁻³ = k · (1/64)
k = 64 × 5×10⁻³ = 0.32
Step 2: Predict at L = 16384
√16384 = 128
L^(-0.5) = 1/128
Δω*(16384) = 0.32 × (1/128) = 0.0025
✅ Answer: k = 0.32, Δω*(16384) = 2.5 × 10⁻³
Doubling √L halves the threshold—classic L^(-1/2) behavior!
Problem 6: Sample Complexity Planning
Read aloud: "Use bound L is greater than C times sigma squared over epsilon squared times log of one over delta. With sigma equals 0.3, epsilon equals 0.05, delta equals 0.05, and C equals 2.0, compute L."
L ≳ C · (σ²/ε²) · log(1/δ)
Step 1: Compute σ²/ε²
σ² = 0.3² = 0.09
ε² = 0.05² = 0.0025
Ratio = 0.09/0.0025 = 36
Step 2: Compute log(1/δ)
1/δ = 1/0.05 = 20
ln(20) ≈ 2.996
Step 3: Multiply by C
L ≳ 2.0 × 36 × 2.996
L ≳ 215.7
✅ Answer: L ≈ 216 → choose L = 256
A few hundred samples suffice for these noise/accuracy levels.
Problem 7: Sub-Bin Peak Refinement (Parabolic)
Read aloud: "Peak at bin k equals 57 with powers P 56 equals 80, P 57 equals 100, P 58 equals 90. Compute parabolic offset delta and refined frequency."
δ = (1/2) · (P_{k-1} - P_{k+1}) / (P_{k-1} - 2P_k + P_{k+1})
ω̂ = (k + δ) / L
Step 1: Compute δ
Numerator: P₅₆ - P₅₈ = 80 - 90 = -10
Denominator: 80 - 2(100) + 90 = -30
δ = (1/2) · (-10/-30) = 1/6 ≈ 0.167
Step 2: Compute refined frequency (L = 16384)
ω̂ = (57 + 0.167) / 16384
= 57.167 / 16384
≈ 0.003489 cycles/sample
✅ Answer: δ ≈ 0.167, ω̂ ≈ 3.489 × 10⁻³
Parabolic interpolation gives sub-bin accuracy!
